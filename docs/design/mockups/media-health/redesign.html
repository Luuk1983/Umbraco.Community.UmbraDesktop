1 · Overview

The bar on top, the rows below, folders last

Your pick: the bar from the story bar direction, with the ring's rows under it. The bar is cut by health, the rows use the same colours, so the top and the actions read as one thing.

  • Check again sits top right as a real button, beside when it was checked. Clean up sits right under the rows it acts on.
  • Rows are ticked by default; untick what you do not trust. Clicking a row opens its grid.
  • Where the space goes answers the other question you come with: not "what can I clean" but "why is it so big". Each folder's bar is split by health too, so a folder that is mostly unused stands out. Clicking a folder opens the grid filtered to it; All folders lists the rest.

4 · One Clean up

For the people who just press clean

You asked whether people will really look at 1,312 orphaned files. Most will not, and every cleaner app is built around that: Windows' Cleanup recommendations, CleanMyMac's Run, Files by Google's Clean. The safety comes from the confirmation saying in plain words what happens to each kind, and from the reversible part being reversible.

  • Unused items go to the recycle bin, so a wrong guess costs a restore, not a re-upload.
  • Orphaned files are the only thing gone for good. A file nobody owns is very rarely missed, and every file is checked again at the moment of deleting, as now.

5 · The grid

A list that still works at 1,312

Today's View is a flat list, fine for 28, useless for 733. This is the Google Photos and Files by Google pattern.

  • Grouped by folder, largest group first, with a checkbox on the group. Clutter comes in folders (an old campaign, an archive), so one tick clears 214 items. That is the real answer to scale.
  • Thumbnails, not rows. Your eye judges 30 images a second; it cannot read 30 file names.
  • The three kinds are chips at the top, so it is one place to work in, not three lists.
  • Groups past the first rows collapse to "+208 more"; the grid only draws what is on screen.
  • Keep… marks items as wanted, with a note and for how long (scene 8). They move to the Kept chip.
  • Grid / One by one switches the same list to Review (scene 7). Review is a way of looking at a list, not a fourth button on the overview.

6 · Instant preview

Quick Look, not a backoffice in a frame

Today a name opens Umbraco's whole media editor in an attached window, which boots a backoffice in an iframe for every look. This is the macOS Quick Look idea: click a thumbnail, and the picture appears over the grid at once. Ticking the checkbox selects; clicking the picture previews.

  • It is one plain image, asked from Umbraco's own image resizer at the window's size (for example 1200 px wide), so a 14 MB photo arrives as a couple of hundred KB.
  • The arrows either side step through the grid, a click outside closes it. Nothing needs a keyboard. PDFs show their first page where the browser can, other files an icon with their details.
  • No editor and no Image Editor here: this is a tool for deciding what goes, not for changing what stays. Show in Media Manager opens a Manager window in the item's folder, with the item selected, like Show in Finder or Show in Explorer.

7 · Review

For the careful editor, and it works

Today the button looks pressable and does nothing: it is disabled in the code, but a disabled primary button does not look disabled in this theme, and "Coming later" only shows on hover. That is a bug either way.

This is the swipe-cleaner idea (Slidebox, Swipewipe, Google Photos' review), done with three big buttons: the picture big, the facts that decide it under it, then Keep, Skip or Trash. The strip shows what is done and what is coming. It is the same Keep as in the grid.

It is reached from the grid's One by one switch, so it works on whatever the grid shows: all unused items, one folder, or just the ones you selected. The same goes for orphaned files and oversized images.

8 · Keep

A reason, and an end date

Keep asks two things, and both have a default so it is still one click: why (optional, empty is fine) and for how long (a year).

  • The note is what makes a keep useful to the next person. Without it, "kept by Luuk" a year later says nothing.
  • The end date is how kept items do not fall off the radar. When it passes, the item is back in the unused list with a small "kept until 27 Oct" badge and the old note, so judging it again takes a second.
  • Always is there for the logo kept for print. It is a deliberate choice, not the default.
  • If a page starts using a kept item, the mark is simply moot. If someone replaces its file, the mark ends, because what was kept is not what is there now.
  • Keep works the same on orphaned files (a file linked straight from a newsletter) and oversized images (a hi-res download meant to be big). Kept items are left out of Clean up.

9 · Kept

Keep management is just a fourth chip

No separate screen: Kept sits next to the three kinds, so you manage keeps where you made them. Every keep shows its note, who kept it and when it ends, the soonest first.

  • Stop keeping puts it back in its own list at once. That is how a colleague overrules a keep: out in the open, with the note in front of them.
  • The overview row says "733 unused, 12 kept", so the keeps are never invisible.
  • Stored in Media Health's own tables, one set per site, beside the check results.
